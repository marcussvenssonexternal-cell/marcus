# The catalogue you already own

_Subscription economics · for Tier-1 streamers, Small–mid streamers (niche and regional), Telco / cable operators_

**Streamers fight churn with new content and new bundles. The cheaper lever is the library they already paid for. Every price rise makes viewers ask whether the service was worth it this month, and that question is settled on the home screen.**

## Why now

Disney+ and Hulu raised prices again on 21 October. Antenna finds about 1 in 3 people who cancel a service buy another within 30 days, so the money stays in streaming and just moves. HUB Research says the service a viewer treats as their default is up to 6x harder to cancel. Being the default is won in discovery, not in the content budget.

## The catalogue you already own is the cheapest answer to churn

_catalogue-01 · On vionlabs.com when Arash approves · Blog post (vionlabs.com) · Industry Trend Commentary_

*Broadcasters are moving their catalogues into other companies' apps. Inside someone else's interface, how each title is described, pictured and previewed is what's left to control.*

The cheapest thing a streaming service can do about churn is make the catalogue it has already paid for easier to find.

TF1 showed what that looks like this summer. Its channels and the TF1+ catalogue went onto Netflix in France in June. Once TF1 titles were part of Netflix's search and recommendations, viewing among the people who saw them rose 140% between July and August, [as reported by Broadband TV News](https://www.broadbandtvnews.com/2026/10/19/tf1-reports-30-viewership-growth-following-netflix-launch/). The two companies pointed to renewed interest in archive programming, including a TF1 series first shown in 2015.

The series itself hadn't changed in eleven years. What was new was the audience in front of it and a system that could recommend it.

On 27 October [Channel 4 agreed to bring its channel and catalogue to Disney+](https://www.channel4.com/press/news/channel-4-and-walt-disney-company-strike-landmark-deal-bring-channel-4-disney) in the UK and Ireland from early 2027, in a Channel 4-branded section with "the same navigation and user experience as the rest of Disney+". I expect more broadcasters to follow, and each of them will find out what TF1 found out: inside someone else's app, a library is worth what that app can understand about it.

## The library is where viewers already are

I think the industry still talks about libraries as a cost line and about originals as the strategy. Viewing points the other way.

Luminate counts 42.2 billion hours of library TV streamed in the US in the first half of 2026, against 11.5 billion hours of original series. Viewers told Luminate they spend nearly 60% of their viewing time on content more than a year old.

Originals still do the job of getting people in the door, and Antenna's sign-up data shows how big a wave a premiere like Landman can bring. A hit ends, though, and what keeps someone subscribed in the months after is mostly the catalogue they find next.

## Every price rise asks whether this month was worth it

Disney+ and Hulu raised prices again in late September, the fourth increase in four years by Variety's count, and existing subscribers pay the new rate from their next bill. Ad-free Disney+ Premium went from $18.99 to $21.49 a month.

Each rise makes people ask whether the service was worth it this month. I believe that question gets answered on the home screen, by whether the viewer found something they wanted in the first minute.

Two numbers show what is at stake:

- HUB Entertainment Research found in September that users of some services are up to six times more likely to keep a service when it is their default.
- Antenna found that 42% of premium streaming cancellations in 2024 came back to the same service within 12 months.

Viewers move between services, and many of them come back. Being the default is what earns the first look, and I'd argue it is won in discovery far more than in the content budget.

## What you keep inside someone else's app

When your catalogue sits in a partner's app, you give up the home screen. The partner owns the rows, the algorithm and the viewing data. What you still control is how each title is described, the artwork that represents it and the preview that plays when someone pauses on it.

![What a catalogue owner still controls inside a partner's app: how each title is described, its mood, theme and tone tags, artwork for every title and episode, and the preview that plays on pause. The home screen, the rows and the algorithm belong to the host.](figures/catalogue-01-controls.png)

The platforms' own partner documentation says as much. Prime Video's artwork guide explains that cover art appears in storefront carousels, "the primary surface driving title discovery", and that when episode art is missing it falls back to season art, which [Amazon describes](https://videocentral.amazon.com/support/getting-started-with-artwork-delivery) as "a suboptimal customer experience". Apple matches partner titles to its own product pages and says artwork matters especially for titles its editorial team promotes.

The titles most likely to arrive with a thin description and one tired image are the old ones, and those are the titles a broadcaster most hopes a new audience will discover. Netflix's research from early 2014 found that artwork made up more than 82% of members' focus while browsing, and that they spent an average of 1.8 seconds on each title. That is very little time for a 2009 drama represented by a grainy press still.

## Describe the old titles the way you describe the new ones

At Vionlabs we see the same pattern in almost every catalogue we work with. Titles came from different distributors in different decades and were described by different people with different vocabularies. A recommender ends up seeing a 2024 original in rich detail and a 2004 film as a genre and a cast list.

[Plex](https://www.vionlabs.com/customer-stories/plex) had exactly that problem: a catalogue gathered from many distributors over decades, described inconsistently in both style and emphasis. We gave every title a consistent layer of genre, keyword and mood data, whatever its origin, era or language, and A/B testing showed a clear uplift in viewing minutes when the mood data was used.

Keeping that layer consistent takes deliberate choices. This autumn we removed 833 terms from the Fingerprint+ vocabularies, because two labels for one idea split the relevant titles across both of them. A filter on "calm" would miss titles tagged "calming".

In a partner's app this matters even more. The host's recommender can only connect your drama to a viewer if it can read what kind of drama it is, in the same terms it uses for everything else on the service.

## The viewer who has watched two titles

The library also matters most for the viewers with the least history. A subscriber who joined yesterday, or a long-tail title nobody has opened in a year, gives a collaborative filter almost nothing to work with.

That is the case we built Curio for. It runs on embeddings learned from more than a million analysed assets, so it can recommend a title on how it feels to watch before anyone has watched it. [Great American Pure Flix is its first platform partner](https://www.vionlabs.com/news/vionlabs-launches-curio) and is bringing it to Personalized Recommendations, Because You Watched and Similar Titles.

## The case against

The strongest objection is that the host decides. A broadcaster can send excellent metadata and the partner may overwrite it, map it to its own taxonomy or keep the titles in a branded corner. TF1's results also come from reach: Netflix puts TF1 in front of far more people than TF1+ could reach on its own, so the value of the shelf and the value of the description are hard to separate.

I agree that reach does most of the work. The 140% came after the titles were fully part of search and recommendations, though, and that is the point where the host's systems have to understand them.

A thin catalogue is a different problem, and better descriptions can't add titles that aren't there. Often, though, the titles are there. Bango found that a quarter of US respondents say they have "run out" of things to watch despite how much is available, and its own reading is that people are running out of patience rather than content.

## Where I'd start

If you own a catalogue, whether it lives in your own app or in someone else's, these numbers tell you whether it is being found:

- The share of your catalogue that got any play in the last 30 days
- Viewing of titles more than five years old, as a share of all viewing
- How many titles are missing episode-level artwork, or a description written in the last few years
- For partner deals, how much of your viewing comes through the host's search and recommendations rather than your branded section

Which of those do you actually know for your own library today?
